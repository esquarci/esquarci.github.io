01 — Project overview

A starting point, not the answer.

Most 0→1 work starts with a founder who already knows exactly what they want to build — the interesting projects are the ones where that conviction turns out to be aimed at the wrong target, and the real job becomes helping them see it without killing their momentum. At Hyper I embedded inside early-stage startups as their product manager, somewhere between a co-founder and a consultant, to help founders work out what they needed to build first.

It sat under “product,” but I stayed close to the craft — driving the UX end to end and working hands-on in the UI, from information architecture and visual hierarchy down to reworking individual screens myself. All of it 0→1: taking a raw idea through discovery, scoping and design into a prototype solid enough to prove the concept and put in front of investors.

Poduca was exactly that kind of project: a confident founder, a crowded market, and twelve weeks to find the version actually worth building.

Context

I embedded as the founder's product manager, working alongside him and Hyper's design team to take a raw, early-stage concept to a working prototype in about twelve weeks.

02 — Discovery

I started with a discovery workshop.

Silas arrived with the solution already decided: an AI editor that would out-feature the established podcast platforms. But when I pushed on why, the reasoning was built on what he imagined creators wanted — faster editing, more AI — rather than anything he'd heard them say. He'd designed for a problem he assumed existed, not one he'd watched anyone actually have.

So before sketching a single screen, my job was to separate the solution he was attached to from the problem worth solving. I ran a discovery workshop built almost entirely around “why” — why editing, why now, who exactly feels this pain, and what they're doing today instead. Each answer that came back as an assumption (“creators find editing too hard”) I reframed as a question we could test (“do they? and is that what actually stops them?”). The goal wasn't to talk him out of his idea — it was to make the gaps in it visible enough that he wanted to go and check.

By the end, the brief had quietly changed shape: instead of “build a better editor,” we'd agreed on something far more useful — we don't actually know where the real friction is yet, so let's find out before we build. That single shift, from defending a solution to investigating a problem, is what made everything after it possible.

03 — Exploration

We ran the research before we ran with the idea.

On a founder's timeline — weeks, not months, with no research budget — the discipline wasn't doing more research, it was choosing the few methods that would move the decision fastest. So before committing to a direction, I ran a focused, four-step research phase, each step deliberately lightweight and each one narrowing in on where Poduca could actually win:

Step 1 · Primary research

Listening to podcasters.

The founder had already surveyed current and aspiring podcasters about why they start, what holds them back, and where the hours go. The appetite was real — but so was the friction. When I researched the wider market, it told the same story: getting started is easy; staying consistent is what defeats most people.

Step 4 · The gap

Plenty of advice. No actual tools.

Look closely at everything before the record button — researching the topic, coming up with ideas, building and holding a posting schedule that actually optimises for views, and writing scripts — and one pattern runs through all of it. There's no shortage of help on how to do it: guides, courses and templates are everywhere, and even the big platforms like Riverside publish detailed advice on writing podcast scripts. But it's all instruction, never assistance — no easy way to automate any of it, or to have something do the heavy lifting with you.

Obstacle
A founder's timeline left no room for deeper research.

Weeks, not months, and no budget for interviews or usability testing — so the discipline was choosing the few methods that would move the decision fastest: the founder's survey for appetite, my own market and competitor research for the white space, and the commercial team's read on where it was all heading. At some point the job was to trust what that showed — three sources pointing the same way was enough to commit to a direction. The deeper questions stayed open, and testing them with real creators is what I'd do next.

Opportunity
Everyone owns a piece of the journey. No one owns the path between them.

The dedicated platforms — Riverside, Alitu, Captivate — cluster around recording, hosting and distribution. The work that actually stops people — ideas, planning, scripting — is left to a general AI chatbot you bolt on yourself, with no link to the tools that come next. The pieces exist; the producing flow that joins them doesn't. That gap was the opening.

04 — The pivot

From “a better editor” to an end-to-end producer.

The research was decisive. Editing, hosting and distribution were crowded, mature markets — entering there meant fighting entrenched players for scraps. The open space was everything before you record: the fragmented, manual, slow producing work no tool owned. So rather than build a better editor, we pivoted Poduca to own that producing layer — and, to their credit, the founder ran with it.

That reframed the whole brief: instead of competing on editing features, Poduca would own the messy producing work upstream of the record button — the slow, manual part of the journey no tool had claimed. The research had turned a fixed product into an open question:

“How might we take a creator from a blank page to a recorded episode — without the days of manual prep?”

The answer became Poduca: an AI co-producer for the whole pre-production journey. It surfaces ideas and trending topics, builds and holds a publishing schedule, drafts and reviews scripts, then guides the recording itself — before handing off to the hosting tools creators already use. One calm flow in place of twelve disconnected steps.

05 — Who it’s for

Who I was designing for.

With the direction set, I turned who-it’s-for into something the design could be measured against. Three groups surfaced in the research — at very different stages, but blocked by the same wall: the effort between an idea and a published episode. The design considerations in each are what I carried into the flow.

06 — Designing the flow

One path that works for a nervous first-timer and a time-poor regular.

With the pivot agreed, I mapped the end-to-end journey, sketched it on Figma, then turned it into hi-fi wireframes — one path that had to carry both a first-timer and a time-poor regular: a clear beginning (an idea), middle (a reviewed script) and end (a recorded episode), without either feeling lost or slowed down.

We worked in high fidelity from the very first wireframe — on purpose. With a founder pitching to investors, low-fi sketches risked confusion about what Poduca actually was, so every screen was detailed enough to be demoed and believed in. The structural UI decisions — consistency, usability, cognitive load — were made here, before anything was styled.

Insight into how I work…

I treat fidelity as a communication decision, not a default. Going hi-fi early is usually the wrong call — but with a founder selling a vision to investors, ambiguity was the real risk. I kept the underlying structure loose — shared components, easy to restyle — so high fidelity bought clarity without locking decisions in too soon.

07 — Feedback & iteration

The screens got sharper in the open.

Designing in high fidelity only works if the people closest to the product can react to it — so I kept the whole flow open to the founder and the commercial team as it came together. Rather than chase verbal notes, I collected feedback directly on the screens with Figma comments, pinned to the exact element in question, so nothing got lost in translation.

Once the comments built up, we ran a short brainstorm to turn scattered reactions into clear decisions: what to keep, what was confusing, and what to refine. The updates that came out of it fed straight back into the screens — the producing flow got tighter every time it was looked at.

Core changes What changed, and why
Change 01 · Podcast plan

Stages and contributors, at a glance.

We added tabs to switch between each stage of an episode — planning, recording, editing — so a show in progress reads as a pipeline rather than one long list. Contributor avatars on each episode show who's worked on the plan, turning a solo tool into a shared workspace the founder's team could actually run together.

Change 02 · Script editor

Cues that travel into the recording.

I added the option to drop a text or audio cue inline as you write — an ad break, a tone note, a sound effect. The point is that they don't stay in the draft: each cue resurfaces on the on-screen script during recording, so the host hits every mark without having to remember it.

Change 03 · Recording session

Eyes on the lens, not the script.

We tried a few layouts before settling on the script overlaid in the centre of the speaker's own frame. Keeping it there means the host's eyeline stays on the camera as they read — so they come across present and natural, not like they're glancing off to read from the side.

Obstacle
Every round of feedback was an invitation to add more.

The founder cared deeply about the detail — which was a gift, but it pulled toward perfecting every screen and bolting on “just one more” feature each review. Part of my job was to keep us anchored to the core producing flow: protecting scope, parking good-but-later ideas, and making sure the prototype stayed focused enough to actually prove the concept.

Insight into how I work…

Feedback is easiest to act on when it's anchored to the thing itself. Pinning comments to the actual screen turned vague reactions into specific, solvable problems — and gave the founder a sense of ownership over a product that was still changing shape.

08 — The interface

An interface you can use without thinking.

This is where the structure from the wireframes became a real, usable product. Working within the brand direction set by Hyper's design team — a confident amber on a dark base — I owned the decisions that make an end-to-end producing platform usable without a manual: a consistent component and navigation system so every screen feels familiar; accessible defaults — a dark theme suited to the low-light spaces podcasters record in, with contrast preserved even when users personalise their accent colour; progressive disclosure that surfaces complexity only when it's needed; and considered states like the empty dashboard a new user first lands on. The result is one calm, coherent producing platform — not a set of separate tools bolted together.

10 — Reflections

What I'd carry forward — and what I'd change.

Designing on a founder's timeline, for an investor audience, means some calls get made for the pitch rather than the product. Looking back honestly, here's where I'd take Poduca next — and what I'd do differently with hindsight.

What I'd do next

Put it in front of real creators.

The prototype proved the vision to investors; the next job is proving the value to the people who'd actually use it. If this were my product, I'd treat the prototype as a research tool, not a finished thing.

  • Run moderated usability tests on the core journey — can a first-timer really get from a blank page to a recorded episode in one sitting?
  • Test whether the AI-generated scripts are trusted unedited, since the whole promise rests on that.
  • Iterate from real signal — watching where people hesitate — rather than founder or investor intuition.
What I'd do differently

Design for the user, not the pitch.

A few decisions were made to show well in a presentation rather than to serve a first MVP. With hindsight, I'd protect scope harder and start the research earlier.

  • Cut the features added purely for the demo — like the live API news feed for dashboard recommendations. It looked impressive to investors but wasn't viable or necessary for a first version.
  • Simplify the busier screens. In places we showed more information than a user actually needs, again driven by what presented well rather than what's usable.
  • Start with usability tests on competitor tools and workshops with potential users far earlier — grounding the direction in real behaviour before designing.
11 — Key learnings

What I'm taking into the next one.

Looking back, the most valuable thing I contributed wasn't any individual screen. It was the discipline of the scoping process itself — helping a founder with a big idea understand that a focused first version isn't a compromise. It's the thing that makes everything else possible.

No. 01

Knowing what to hand off is a design decision.

Poduca could have chased recording, editing and hosting too. The discipline that made it work was owning one layer — producing — and deliberately handing the rest to the tools creators already trust. Drawing the product's boundary mattered as much as any screen: a focused first version beats a worse copy of five mature products.

No. 02

One path, two very different users.

Maya had never made an episode; Sam makes one every week and resents the admin around it. The hard constraint was that both needed the same guided flow to feel right — encouraging to one, fast to the other — without branching into two products. Designing a single journey that flexes to opposite needs is something I'll reach for in any product with a mixed user base.

No. 03

With AI, the real design problem is trust.

Poduca's whole promise rests on people trusting AI-generated ideas and scripts enough to actually use them. So the design work wasn't generating content — it was making the AI's output reviewable and correctable: scores, accept/reject suggestions, a script you check before you record. Designing an AI product is mostly designing the moments where the human stays in control.
